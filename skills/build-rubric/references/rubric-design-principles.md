## ANU Institutional Guidance

Source: ANU Learning & Teaching Centre, "Designing and Using Rubrics"
collection (9 pages), https://learningandteaching.anu.edu.au/resources/

### Rubric types (2×2 matrix)

|  | Task-specific | General |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytic** | Separate scores per criterion; specific to one task | Separate scores; reusable across tasks |
| **Holistic** | Single overall score; specific to one task | Single overall score; reusable |

**Default recommendation:** Analytic, task-specific. Use analytic when:
- Multiple criteria need separate evaluation
- Specific feedback is important
- Peer review is involved
- Multiple markers are involved

### Performance levels

ANU standard grading scale:

| Grade | Range | Standard |
|-------|-------|----------|
| HD | 80–100 | Exceptional |
| D | 70–79 | Superior |
| Cr | 60–69 | Good |
| P | 50–59 | Satisfactory |
| N | 0–49 | Not demonstrated |

For smaller tasks (10–15%), collapsed bands (HD–D, Cr–P, N) may
suffice.

### Criteria

- 3–6 criteria recommended (too many overwhelms; too few prevents
  valid judgements)
- Criteria should align with CLOs
- Criteria should NOT contain quality language (quality goes in
  descriptors)
- Criteria can be weighted to signal priority

### Descriptor writing (ANU + Brookhart 2018 + Melbourne TLI)

1. **Descriptive, not evaluative.** Describe what the work looks like.
   - Bad: "Excellent literature review"
   - Good: "Synthesises relevant literature to identify a clear gap"

2. **Strengths-based.** Lower levels describe what IS present.
   - Bad: "Fails to identify a research gap"
   - Good: "Identifies some relevant sources but does not yet
     synthesise them into a coherent argument for the research gap"

3. **Parallel structure.** Every level addresses the same dimensions.

4. **Action verb differentiation.** Verbs are the key differentiator:

   | Level | Verb examples |
   |-------|--------------|
   | HD | Synthesises, evaluates, integrates, constructs |
   | D | Analyses, compares, applies, demonstrates |
   | Cr | Describes, identifies, explains, applies |
   | P | Lists, summarises, reproduces, recognises |
   | N | Names, attempts, gestures toward |

5. **Observable and measurable.** "What would I see in the text?"

6. **Start from HD and work downward.**

### Step-by-step design process (ANU recommended)

1. Choose rubric type (decision tree)
2. Outline criteria aligned with CLOs
3. Select performance level approach
4. Develop descriptors (start from HD, work down)
5. Revise and reflect on effectiveness

### Recommended practices timeline

- **Before semester:** Align criteria with CLOs
- **Before introducing assessment:** Use rubric in class discussion;
  provide for self/peer assessment practice
- **During assessment:** Share rubric on LMS before students start;
  review in class
- **After submission:** Supplement rubric with additional comments;
  collect exemplars for moderation

## AI Assessment Scale (Perkins et al. 2024)

Source: https://aiassessmentscale.com/ — endorsed by TEQSA.

Five levels of permitted AI integration per task:

| Level | Name | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| 1 | No AI | Completed without AI assistance |
| 2 | AI Planning | AI for brainstorming, outlining, initial research |
| 3 | AI Collaboration | AI assists drafting, feedback, refinement; student evaluates and modifies |
| 4 | Full AI | Extensive AI throughout; student directs strategically |
| 5 | AI Exploration | Creative, experimental co-creation |

**Specify the AIAS level for each assessment task.** Most postgraduate
coursework assessments with responsible AI use will be Level 3.

## TEQSA Principles (Australian regulatory context)

1. Assessment should equip students to participate ethically in an
   AI-ubiquitous society
2. Trustworthy judgements require multiple, inclusive, contextualised
   assessment approaches
3. Skilful AI use is a positive competency, not merely an integrity
   risk

## Dual-Use Design (marker + peer reviewer)

When rubrics are used for peer review:

- Language must be accessible to non-expert assessors
- Provide glossaries for specialist terms
- Provide criterion-specific guidance where peer reviewers lack
  disciplinary expertise
- Consider a calibration exercise before peer review begins
- Distinguish evaluative from developmental feedback — peers are
  often better at identifying strengths and asking questions than
  assigning scores

## Common Pitfalls in Humanities Research Proposal Rubrics

1. **Vague descriptors** — "demonstrates sophisticated understanding"
   without specifying what sophistication looks like
2. **Conflating dimensions** — a single criterion for "research design
   and methodology" that encompasses question, method, justification,
   feasibility, and ethics
3. **Over-emphasis on surface features** — weighting formatting and
   referencing style heavily
4. **Ignoring argumentative structure** — assessing components without
   assessing the coherence of the overall argument (the "red thread")
5. **Rubrics too generic for the task** — not capturing what matters
   in the specific discipline
6. **No distinction between proposal quality and project feasibility**
7. **Rating scales without descriptors** — 1–5 without describing each
   level

## Teaching Callout Technique

Blockquoted callout boxes placed between criterion description and
descriptor table. Pattern:

1. **Name the skill** that distinguishes quality levels
2. **Explain it concretely** with a before/after example drawn from
   the cohort's own topics (not generic placeholders)
3. **Point to the course tool** that teaches or practises the skill

Target the Cr/D boundary. Creates a dual structure: descriptor tables
= assessment instrument, callout boxes = teaching instrument.

**Using real examples:** Draw from student pre-survey data (declared
topics, research interests). Students recognise their own concerns,
creating relevance and social proof. Spread examples across multiple
students' topics so no one feels singled out.

**Connecting to course tools:** Name specific prompts, exercises, or
frameworks from the course. Creates a feedback loop: students learn
both what is wrong and which tool to use to fix it.

## Named Transferable Concepts

When a quality distinction recurs, give it a name. Named concepts
become shared vocabulary, make feedback more precise, and help
students self-diagnose. Examples from the HUMN8031 rubric:

| Concept | Meaning | Diagnostic use |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| **Red thread** (*der rote Faden*) | The continuous argument connecting all sections | "Can I pull out any section and the rest still makes sense? If so, the red thread is missing." |
| **Cosmetic satisficing** | LLM improves surface polish but structural problems remain | "Polished prose with a circular argument = cosmetic satisficing." |
| **Assert vs. demonstrate** | Claiming significance vs. grounding it in evidence | "Does the significance section say 'this is understudied' or show specifically what existing work does not address?" |
| **Topic vs. problem** | Area of study vs. what is unresolved within it | "Is the question a topic restated in interrogative form, or does it arise from an identified gap?" |
| **Name vs. plan** | Naming a method vs. explaining what you will do with it | "'I will use discourse analysis' names a tool. 'I will collect posts from three hashtags and analyse how users frame political imagery' explains a plan." |

## The "What Would Tools Have Caught" Lens

For tool-use or process criteria, frame peer reviewer indicators as
weaknesses that collaboration with course tools would likely have
caught. For each indicator, name the specific tool:

| Observable weakness | Tool that would have caught it |
|---|---|
| Circular argument, unsupported claims | Writing self-review prompt |
| Unrealistic timeline, unacknowledged constraints | Feasibility audit prompt |
| Topic restated as question (no problem identified) | Research question refinement prompt |
| Significance asserted without evidence | Self-review prompt |
| Sources listed individually without connection | Source-questioning grid (Chapter 3) |

This framing does three things:
1. Makes the criterion *assessable* — peer reviewers know what to
   look for
2. Creates a *feedback loop* — students learn which tool to use
3. Reinforces the *value of course tools* — they are not optional
   extras but directly connected to how work is evaluated

## Process Statement Design

For multi-assessment sequences where tool use is assessed:

**First assessment:** Assess tool use inferentially from observable
quality indicators (no process statement required). This avoids adding
requirements after the description is published.

**Later assessments:** Add a lightweight process statement:
- 150–250 words, appended after references, excluded from word count
- Two focused questions: (1) which tools (give examples — Zotero,
  Google Scholar, Scopus, Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, The Forge,
  Connected Papers, etc., including tools not introduced in the
  course), (2) most significant way a tool shaped the work
- Optional reflective sub-question: where did you rely on own
  judgement? what would you do differently?
- Tone: honest and specific, reflection not justification

**Avoid:** Exhaustive stage-by-stage inventories. Two focused questions
yield more diagnostic information than "describe your workflow at each
stage," which invites performative documentation.

## Assessment Description Improvement Patterns

The rubric-building process reliably reveals these friction points in
assessment descriptions. Check for and recommend fixes:

1. **Description says "describe"; rubric rewards "justify"** — add
   "and explain why this approach is appropriate"
2. **Components conflated** — separate aims from questions, framework
   from background
3. **Implicit expectations** — if the rubric assesses something (e.g.,
   sequencing, dependencies), the description should mention it
4. **Tool requirements vague** — "recommended" should be "required"
   if the rubric penalises non-use
5. **Specialist terms undefined** — if the rubric needs a glossary,
   the description should include a definition too
6. **Significance not grounded** — add "ground your argument in
   specific gaps in existing literature, not personal interest"
