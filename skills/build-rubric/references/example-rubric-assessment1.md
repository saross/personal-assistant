# Assessment 1: Research Proposal — Marking Rubric (DRAFT v2)

**Assessment:** Research Proposal (20%, 1000 words excluding references
and figures)
**Course:** HUMN8031 Digital Humanities Research Project, Semester 1 2026
**Learning Outcomes Assessed:** CLO 1 (design), CLO 2
**AI Assessment Scale Level:** 3 — AI Collaboration (AI assists with
drafting, feedback, and refinement; student critically evaluates and
modifies outputs)

> **Note on CLO 1.** CLO 1 reads "design and execute an independent
> research project." At the proposal stage, only the *design* component
> is assessable. The *execute* component is assessed in Assessments 2
> and 3.

---

## Minimum standards (gating requirements)

These are not graduated criteria. Submissions that do not meet these
minimum standards will be returned for revision or capped at a Pass (P)
grade, regardless of quality in other criteria.

- [ ] Submitted via Canvas by the deadline (or with approved extension)
- [ ] Within the word count (1000 words ± 10%, excluding references and
  figures)
- [ ] Includes a reference list using a recognised, consistently applied
  referencing style
- [ ] Includes a Gantt chart (as a figure, excluded from the word count)
- [ ] Written in English (drafting and thinking in other languages is
  encouraged; the submitted text must be in English)

## Rubric

### Criterion 1: Research Problem, Question, and Aims (20%)

*CLO 1. Does the proposal articulate a clear research problem grounded
in a genuine gap or tension, frame a focused question, and state what
the project aims to achieve?*

> **The topic-vs-problem distinction.** A *topic* tells us an area
> ("AI and historical maps"). A *problem* tells us what is unresolved
> ("historical maps contain archaeological data locked in scanned images,
> and current extraction methods are inadequate"). The research question
> refinement prompt from Week 2 is designed to help you move from one to
> the other. If your question still reads as a topic in interrogative
> form, return to that prompt.

| HD (80–100) | D (70–79) | Cr (60–69) | P (50–59) | N (0–49) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Articulates a precise research problem that identifies what is missing, incomplete, or unresolved in current understanding. The research question is narrowly scoped, answerable within the project's constraints, and arises directly from the problem. Aims or objectives are stated and connect logically to the question. The working title captures the project's focus and scope. | Articulates a clear research problem and frames a focused research question that connects logically to the problem. Aims are stated, though they may overlap with the question rather than extending it. The relationship between problem and question is evident, though the question may be slightly broad or the problem could be stated more precisely. The title is informative. | Identifies a research area and frames a question, but the specific gap or tension — what is missing, incomplete, or broken in current understanding — is implicit rather than stated. The question is answerable but its connection to a problem is underdeveloped. Aims may be vague or indistinguishable from the question. The title is descriptive but generic. | States a research topic and poses a question, but the question reads as a topic restated in interrogative form rather than arising from an identified problem. Aims restate the question rather than specifying what the project will achieve. The title names the topic. | Names a broad area of interest but does not formulate a research problem, question, or aims. The reader cannot determine what the project proposes to investigate. |

### Criterion 2: Contextual Framework and Scholarly Engagement (20%)

*CLO 2. Does the proposal situate the research within relevant fields,
debates, and theoretical or analytical frameworks?*

**Glossary for peer reviewers:** A *theoretical or analytical framework*
is the lens through which you examine your research question — for
example, a concept from your field (e.g., "platformisation,"
"remediation," "actor-network theory"), a methodological tradition
(e.g., "discourse analysis," "grounded theory"), or a set of principles
that guides how you interpret evidence. It shapes what you look for and
how you make sense of what you find.

> **Connecting sources, not just listing them.** The difference between
> a Credit and a Distinction in this criterion is often the difference
> between presenting sources individually and connecting them to each
> other and to your research problem. The source-questioning grid from
> Chapter 3 practises exactly this skill: "What does this source lead me
> to next?" and "What broader questions does it connect to?" If your
> contextual framework reads as a list of summaries, revisit those
> questions.

| HD (80–100) | D (70–79) | Cr (60–69) | P (50–59) | N (0–49) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Situates the research within relevant disciplinary fields and identifies specific debates, approaches, or schools of thought that inform the project. Names a theoretical or analytical framework and explains how it will guide the research. Identifies tensions, limitations, or gaps in the cited sources rather than simply reporting what others have found. Sources are connected to each other and to the research problem, building toward a rationale for the project. | Situates the research within a relevant field and identifies a theoretical or analytical framework. Engages with several sources that are relevant to the research problem, though the engagement describes what sources say more than it evaluates how they relate to the research gap. The connection between framework and research question is stated. | Identifies a relevant field and references some sources, but the contextual framework reads as background information rather than a scholarly rationale for the research. A theoretical or analytical framework is named but not explained, or its relevance to the question is unclear. Sources are presented individually rather than connected to each other. | References some literature and positions it in relation to the topic area, but sources are listed or summarised rather than connected to the research problem. The relationship between the cited work and the proposed research is implicit. No theoretical or analytical framework is identified. | Cites no relevant literature, or the sources cited are unrelated to the stated research problem. The proposal does not attempt to position the research within existing scholarship. |

### Criterion 3: Significance and Contribution (20%)

*CLO 1. Can the student argue why this research matters — what gap it
addresses, what contribution it makes?*

> **Assert vs. demonstrate.** A common pattern in academic writing is to
> assert that a topic is important without demonstrating *why*. Compare:
>
> *Assertion:* "Museum digitisation is an understudied area."
>
> *Demonstration:* "Existing studies of museum digitisation focus on
> Western institutions and assume voluntary adoption, but none examines
> how Chinese war museums navigate the tension between political
> narrative control and digital accessibility — a gap that matters
> because these museums serve both domestic commemoration and
> international diplomacy."
>
> Moving from assertion to demonstration is a specific, learnable skill.
> The writing self-review prompt flags unsupported claims; use it to test
> whether your significance argument is grounded in evidence.

| HD (80–100) | D (70–79) | Cr (60–69) | P (50–59) | N (0–49) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Builds a specific, evidence-supported argument for the significance of the research by identifying a gap, tension, or unresolved question in existing literature or practice. Explains what the project will contribute — whether new knowledge, a new perspective, a new method, or a new application — and articulates why that contribution matters beyond the student's own interest. | Identifies a gap or need that the research addresses and explains what the project will contribute. The argument for significance is clear, though it relies more on asserting importance ("the digital transformation of museums is understudied") than demonstrating it with reference to specific sources or debates. | States that the research is significant but the argument is general or formulaic ("little research has been done on technologically mediated performance in the Spring Festival Gala"). The gap is implied rather than demonstrated through engagement with the literature. The contribution is described in broad terms without specifics about what new understanding the project will produce. | Claims the topic is important and gestures toward a reason, but the reason is personal interest or topical relevance rather than a gap in existing knowledge. The contribution is framed as "more research on this topic" rather than a specific outcome. | Does not address significance, or the stated significance is unrelated to the research problem described elsewhere in the proposal. |

### Criterion 4: Research Design and Feasibility (20%)

*CLO 1. Does the proposal outline an appropriate methodology, identify
data sources, and demonstrate realistic project planning?*

> **Naming a method vs. explaining a plan.** "I will use discourse
> analysis" names a tool but does not explain a plan. "I will collect
> posts from three Xiaohongshu hashtags over a six-month period and
> analyse them using discourse analysis to identify how users frame
> political imagery" explains what you will do, what you will examine,
> and why the method fits the question. The feasibility audit prompt is
> designed to push you from the first to the second — it asks what data
> you need, whether you can access it, and whether your timeline is
> realistic.

| HD (80–100) | D (70–79) | Cr (60–69) | P (50–59) | N (0–49) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outlines a methodology appropriate to the research question and explains why this approach suits the question. Identifies data sources and analytical methods with enough specificity to be actionable. The Gantt chart decomposes the project into sequenced phases with realistic durations, marks the three assessment deadlines as milestones, and shows how tasks relate to each other. The plan acknowledges at least one constraint or risk and how it will be managed. | Outlines a methodology appropriate to the research question with identified data sources and methods. The Gantt chart shows project phases with approximate durations, includes the assessment deadlines, and reflects a realistic sense of sequencing. The plan is feasible, though the connection between methodology and question could be made more explicit. Feasibility constraints are acknowledged, at least implicitly. | Describes a general approach and names methods or data sources, but the methodology lacks specificity — the reader cannot determine exactly what the student will do or analyse. The Gantt chart shows phases and durations but the sequencing may be unrealistic, or key phases (e.g., analysis, revision) are missing. Feasibility is not addressed. | Names a method or approach and lists some tasks, but the method is not connected to the research question. The Gantt chart is present but minimal — tasks are listed without meaningful sequencing or duration estimates. The plan does not reflect the project decomposition practised in class. | Names a broad area of interest but does not describe a methodology or analytical approach. The Gantt chart is absent, or it is present but uninformative (e.g., a single bar labelled "research"). |

### Criterion 5: Argumentative Coherence and Communication (10%)

*CLOs 1 + 2. Does the proposal hold together as a connected scholarly
argument? Is the writing clear and well-structured?*

> **The red thread** (*der rote Faden*). This metaphor comes from German
> literary tradition — a red thread woven through a text that connects
> every part to a single, continuous argument. In a strong proposal, the
> problem motivates the question, the question shapes the framework, the
> framework informs the methodology, and the significance connects back
> to the gap identified at the start. Each section builds on the previous
> one rather than standing alone. If a reader can pull out any section
> and the remaining sections still make sense independently, the red
> thread is missing — you have written four separate sections, not one
> connected argument.

| HD (80–100) | D (70–79) | Cr (60–69) | P (50–59) | N (0–49) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The proposal reads as a connected argument with a clear red thread: the problem motivates the question, the question shapes the framework, the framework informs the methodology, and the significance connects back to the gap identified at the start. Prose is clear and well-organised, using formal scholarly language suitable for a research proposal. Transitions between sections build the argument rather than merely separating topics. | The proposal is well-organised and the components connect logically. The reader can trace a line from problem to design, though some transitions between sections are abrupt or the connections could be made more explicit. The red thread is present but not consistently maintained. Writing is clear and largely free of errors that impede comprehension. | The proposal addresses all required components but they read as separate sections rather than parts of a connected argument. The reader can follow each section individually but the overall logic — why this question, approached this way, matters — is not fully articulated. Writing is generally clear. | The proposal contains the required components but the connections between them are difficult to follow. Sections may appear in an unclear order, or the reader must infer how the question relates to the methodology. Writing contains errors or ambiguities that impede comprehension in places. | The proposal is missing major components or is disorganised to the point that the reader cannot determine what research is being proposed. Writing quality prevents comprehension. |

### Criterion 6: Research Process and Tool Use (10%)

*Course-specific. Does the proposal show evidence of a deliberate
research workflow, including effective use of LLM tools as taught in
the course?*

> **Note on assessment context.** This is the first assessed piece of
> work in the course. Students are developing proficiency with research
> tools and LLM workflows. The criterion distinguishes between students
> who are engaging with these tools and developing their practice
> (which is expected) and students who show no evidence of engagement
> (which is a concern). Mastery is not expected at this stage.

**Guidance for peer reviewers and markers:** You are assessing what you
can observe in the submitted work — you do not need to know which
specific tools were used. Look for observable indicators, particularly
weaknesses that collaboration with an LLM would likely have caught
using the course's grimoire prompts:

- Does the argument show signs of having been **reviewed and revised**
  (substantive improvements to structure and logic), or does it read
  like a first draft that has only been spell-checked? *A polished
  surface with a circular argument suggests cosmetic editing only; rough
  prose with a tight logical structure suggests substantive revision.*
  The writing self-review prompt is designed to flag exactly these
  structural issues — circular arguments, unsupported claims, logical
  gaps.
- Are references **consistently formatted** (suggesting a reference
  manager) or inconsistent (suggesting manual entry)?
- Does the Gantt chart reflect **genuine planning** (sequenced phases,
  realistic durations) or a last-minute addition? An unrealistic
  timeline or unacknowledged constraints are what the feasibility audit
  prompt is designed to surface.
- Does the research question show signs of **refinement** (a precise
  problem, not a generic topic)? A question that is still a topic
  restated in interrogative form suggests the research question
  refinement prompt was not used, or was not used to completion.
- Does the significance section **demonstrate** a gap, or merely
  **assert** importance? The self-review prompt specifically checks
  whether claims are supported by evidence.

| HD (80–100) | D (70–79) | Cr (60–69) | P (50–59) | N (0–49) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The proposal shows clear evidence of a deliberate research workflow. The argument has the hallmarks of substantive review — structural and logical issues have been addressed, not just surface polish. The Gantt chart reflects genuine project decomposition. References are managed consistently. The research question and problem statement suggest engagement with the coaching and refinement process taught in class. The proposal demonstrates that LLMs were used for substantive tasks (e.g., argument critique, feasibility testing) rather than only surface editing such as grammar correction or rephrasing. | The proposal shows evidence of a structured research process. The work has been reviewed and revised beyond a first draft — the argument is coherent and the components are connected. References are consistently formatted. The Gantt chart shows thoughtful planning. There may be missed opportunities where course tools could have strengthened the work — for example, a methodology that the feasibility audit prompt could have stress-tested, a significance argument that the self-review prompt would have pushed beyond assertion, or a research question that further refinement would have sharpened. | The proposal shows uneven evidence of tool use. Some components suggest careful preparation (e.g., a well-formatted Gantt chart or a refined research question) while others suggest a first draft (e.g., an argument that circles back on itself, or a significance section that asserts rather than demonstrates). Referencing is present but inconsistent. The student is engaging with course tools but has not yet applied them across the full proposal. | The proposal reads as an early draft that has been through surface editing (grammar, spelling) but not substantive review of argument or structure. The Gantt chart appears to have been added as an afterthought. Referencing is inconsistent or minimal. The student may be engaging with course tools at a basic level, but the proposal does not yet reflect the research workflow taught in class. | The proposal shows no observable evidence of engagement with research tools or a structured research process. Components appear to have been composed without planning, review, or revision. |

## Instructions for peer reviewers

You are using the same rubric as the course convenor. Your role is to
provide honest, specific feedback — not to assign a final grade. When
reviewing a classmate's proposal:

1. **Read the full proposal once** before assessing any criterion.
2. **For each criterion**, identify what the proposal does well and what
   could be improved. Use the descriptor language in the rubric to guide
   your observations — it tells you what to look for at each quality
   level.
3. **Be specific.** "The research question is clear" is less helpful
   than "The question identifies a specific gap — the lack of
   computational methods for extracting data from historical maps — and
   frames an answerable question about whether VLMs can fill that gap."
4. **Focus on the work, not the person.** "The significance section
   asserts importance without demonstrating a gap" is constructive.
   "You didn't try hard enough" is not.
5. **Criterion 2 (Contextual Framework):** You do not need expertise in
   your classmate's field. Focus on whether sources are *connected to
   each other and to the research problem*, or whether they are listed
   individually without explanation of how they relate.
6. **Criterion 4 (Research Design):** You are not assessing whether the
   method is the *best* choice — that requires specialist knowledge.
   Focus on whether the student *explains* their method, whether the
   Gantt chart looks realistic, and whether you can follow the plan.
7. **Criterion 6 (Research Process and Tool Use):** Focus on observable
   quality indicators — see the detailed guidance above the Criterion 6
   descriptor table, which lists specific weaknesses the course prompts
   are designed to catch.

## Instructions for automated evaluation (Claude)

When evaluating a submission against this rubric:

1. **Assess each criterion independently.** Do not let strength in one
   area compensate for weakness in another.
2. **Use the descriptor language as a checklist.** For each criterion,
   identify which performance level best describes the submission. If
   the work falls between two levels, assign the lower level and note
   what would move it up.
3. **Quote specific passages** from the submission to support your
   assessment. Every claim about quality should be grounded in evidence
   from the text.
4. **For Criterion 6**, assess observable indicators:
   - Does the argument structure suggest substantive review (structural
     and logical improvements, not just grammar)?
   - Are references consistently formatted (suggesting a reference
     manager)?
   - Does the Gantt chart show genuine decomposition (sequenced phases,
     dependencies, realistic durations)?
   - Does the research question show signs of refinement (precise
     problem statement, not a generic topic)?
   - Are there missed opportunities where course tools could have
     improved the work (e.g., a significance argument that asserts
     rather than demonstrates, suggesting the self-review prompt was
     not used for that section)?
   - Is there evidence of cosmetic satisficing — polished prose that
     masks structural problems (circular arguments, unsupported claims,
     logical gaps)?
5. **Gantt chart limitation.** If the Gantt chart is submitted as an
   image, you may not be able to evaluate its content. In that case,
   assess what you can infer from the proposal text (e.g., does the
   methodology section reference the timeline? does the text mention
   phases, sequencing, or dependencies?) and note the limitation.
6. **Provide an overall assessment** with:
   - A grade for each criterion (HD/D/Cr/P/N with a specific mark)
   - A weighted overall grade
   - Three specific strengths
   - Three specific areas for improvement, each tied to a criterion and
     descriptor level
